Colonial Harm, Broken Bonds, and the Politics of Survival in The Strangers

A postcolonial reading of Katherena Vermette's novel as a story of family rupture, cultural memory, and Indigenous women's resistance.

The Strangers

Author: katherena vermette

Lens: Postcolonial theory

Focus: family separation, cultural memory, gendered colonial violence, survival

Core Argument

Thesis

From a postcolonial perspective, Katherena Vermette's The Strangers shows that family disconnection is a lasting effect of colonial power, while reconnecting with family, preserving cultural memory, and surviving as Indigenous women all serve as acts of resistance against colonial harm.

01

Family Bonds Are Broken by Systems

The novel treats broken family relationships not as isolated personal failure, but as part of the larger history of colonial policy and child welfare intervention.

02

Memory Becomes Resistance

Remembering language, stories, relatives, and intergenerational knowledge becomes an active refusal of cultural erasure.

03

Survival Is Political

The continued presence of Indigenous women challenges settler-colonial attempts to erase Indigenous families, bodies, and futures.

Novel Context

Why the story matters

The Strangers follows several generations of women in the Stranger family. The novel's fragmented, multi-voice structure mirrors the family disconnection it represents: mothers, daughters, grandmothers, and children are pulled apart by violence, addiction, social systems, and inherited trauma.

Instead of presenting trauma as only private pain, Vermette connects individual suffering to public history. The family's struggle to reconnect is therefore not only emotional; it is also cultural and political.

High-score reading move

The website does not simply summarize the plot. It connects narrative form, historical context, and literary theory to prove a focused thesis.

Character Map

The Stranger women

The novel's emotional power comes from how several generations of women experience separation differently, but remain connected by family history, memory, and survival.

Cedar

Cedar's distance from her mother, sister, and earlier family history makes her a central figure for understanding how colonial harm interrupts belonging.

Elsie

Elsie's struggle with addiction and reconnection shows how trauma can move through families while still leaving room for responsibility and care.

Phoenix

Phoenix's experience with confinement and motherhood connects personal pain to wider systems that police and separate Indigenous women.

Margaret

Margaret's memories and relationships show how older generations carry unresolved grief, but also hold pieces of family knowledge.

Literary Theory

Postcolonial Lens

Postcolonial theory asks how colonial power continues after formal colonial moments seem to be "over." In this reading, colonialism survives through institutions, family separation, cultural loss, and unequal access to safety.

Settler colonialism seeks to replace Indigenous presence.

Patrick Wolfe's theory of settler colonialism helps explain why the novel's broken family bonds matter. If colonial power tries to weaken Indigenous continuity, then reconnecting relatives and protecting future generations becomes resistance.

Memory is more than nostalgia.

In the novel, remembering a word, a relationship, a place, or a story becomes a living practice. Cultural memory keeps knowledge moving across generations even when institutions attempt to interrupt that movement.

Colonial violence is also gendered.

The National Inquiry into MMIWG connects violence against Indigenous women, girls, and 2SLGBTQQIA+ people to colonial and patriarchal policies. This context deepens the stakes of Vermette's portrayal of Indigenous women's vulnerability and endurance.

Evidence Map

How each body section supports the thesis

1. Family separation

Claim: The rupture between mothers, daughters, and grandmothers is connected to colonial systems rather than simple personal weakness.

Historical support: UBC Indigenous Foundations explains that the Sixties Scoop involved the mass removal of Indigenous children from families into child welfare systems, often without family or band consent.

Novel link: The Stranger family's gaps and silences echo this history of forced disconnection.

2. Cultural memory

Claim: Remembering family, language, and story is a form of resistance.

Literary support: The multi-generational structure makes Indigenous women knowledge-holders instead of passive subjects of institutions.

Novel link: The website presents memory as an everyday practice that fights erasure.

3. Survival as resistance

Claim: In the context of colonial and gendered violence, survival itself becomes political.

Contextual support: The National Inquiry describes how colonial and patriarchal policies displaced Indigenous women from traditional community roles and increased vulnerability to violence.

Novel link: The Stranger women's endurance challenges the assumption that colonial harm has the final word.

Historical Context

From policy to family experience

1950s-60s

Residential school systems begin to shift, but child welfare systems expand their control over Indigenous families.

1960s-80s

The Sixties Scoop removes many Indigenous children from families and communities, producing lasting identity and kinship loss.

Today

Reports on MMIWG show that colonial harm continues through institutions, social vulnerability, and justice-system failures.

Novel

The Strangers turns this history into intimate family narrative, asking readers to see survival as an active refusal of erasure.

Recording script

In The Strangers, Katherena Vermette does not present survival as something simple or inspirational. She shows that survival is difficult, uneven, and political. Through the Stranger women, the novel reveals how colonial systems can break family bonds, disconnect people from culture, and make Indigenous women more vulnerable to violence and silence. However, the novel also shows that memory, reconnection, and storytelling are forms of resistance. When characters try to remember family, protect younger generations, or hold on to cultural knowledge, they challenge the colonial logic that attempted to erase them. My website uses a postcolonial lens to show that the Stranger women are not only victims of harm. They are also knowledge-keepers, survivors, and resistors. Their continued presence becomes a powerful answer to colonial violence.

Conclusion

What the novel finally argues

Vermette's novel does not allow colonial violence to become the final meaning of the Stranger women's lives. Their relationships are damaged, but not meaningless; their memories are interrupted, but not destroyed; their survival is painful, but powerful. Through a postcolonial lens, The Strangers becomes a story about how Indigenous women continue to resist erasure by remaining present, remembering, and reaching toward one another.
