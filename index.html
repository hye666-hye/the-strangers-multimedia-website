About the Novel

Novel overview

The Strangers is an intergenerational novel about the Stranger family, especially the women whose relationships have been shaped by separation, trauma, and the ongoing effects of colonial systems.

Title: The StrangersAuthor: Katherena VermettePublished: 2021Publisher: Hamish HamiltonGenre: Literary fiction

Author and genre

Katherena Vermette is a M?tis writer from Winnipeg. The novel is literary fiction that uses multiple voices and family history to explore identity, memory, and survival.

Setting

The story is connected to contemporary Winnipeg and Treaty 1 territory, as well as social spaces such as family homes, child welfare systems, detention, and community networks. These settings make colonial harm feel present rather than distant.

Plot summary

The novel follows several generations of the Stranger family as Cedar, Elsie, Phoenix, Margaret, and other relatives struggle with separation, addiction, motherhood, grief, and the need to reconnect. Instead of one simple plotline, the story moves through different voices to show how trauma and memory pass across generations.

Main themes

Major themes include family disconnection, Indigenous identity, cultural memory, gendered colonial violence, motherhood, survival, and resistance. These themes connect directly to the postcolonial lens used throughout the website.

Author Introduction

Katherena Vermette

Katherena Vermette is a Metis writer from Treaty 1 territory, Winnipeg, Manitoba. Her writing often connects family, place, memory, Indigenous identity, and the ongoing effects of colonial violence.

Knowing Vermette's background matters because The Strangers is not written as a distant social issue. It is a literary work grounded in Indigenous women's voices, intergenerational relationships, and the realities of Winnipeg and the Canadian colonial context.

Core Argument

Thesis

From a postcolonial perspective, Katherena Vermette's The Strangers shows that family disconnection is a lasting effect of colonial power, while reconnecting with family, preserving cultural memory, and surviving as Indigenous women all serve as acts of resistance against colonial harm.

01

Family separation

Broken relationships are connected to colonial systems, including child welfare and the history of the Sixties Scoop.

02

Cultural memory

Memory, language, and family stories become ways of resisting erasure and rebuilding identity.

03

Political survival

Survival is not passive. Indigenous women's continued presence challenges the colonial logic of elimination.

Plot and Character Guide

Who the story follows

The novel uses several connected perspectives, so the characters are important to understanding the plot and the thesis.

Cedar

Cedar's distance from her mother, sister, and wider family history shows how colonial harm can interrupt belonging. Her story helps introduce the website's focus on identity and reconnection.

Elsie

Elsie is Cedar's mother. Her struggle with addiction, care, and family separation shows how trauma can move through generations without erasing the desire for connection.

Phoenix

Phoenix's experience with confinement and motherhood connects the novel to systems that police, isolate, and judge Indigenous women.

Margaret

Margaret represents an older generation carrying grief, memory, and family history. Her role helps show how the past continues to shape the present.

Direct Quotations + Commentary

Short textual evidence

"She missed her mother every day" (Vermette 47)

This quote shows that family separation is not abstract; it is felt through daily loss. In a postcolonial reading, the missing mother-daughter bond reflects how colonial systems damage kinship and identity.

"survival of peoples survival of languages survival of knowledges" (Vermette 197)

This phrase connects survival to culture, language, and knowledge. It supports the thesis because remembering and preserving cultural memory become acts of resistance against erasure.

"she was still here, she was still standing" (Vermette 312)

This quote turns survival into a political statement. The character's continued presence challenges colonial violence by showing that harm does not get the final word.

Theory Lens

Postcolonial interpretation

Colonialism continues through systems.

Patrick Wolfe's theory helps explain why broken family bonds matter. If settler colonialism attempts to weaken Indigenous continuity, then rebuilding family and memory becomes resistance.

Memory is an active practice.

Remembering names, relatives, language, stories, and history keeps Indigenous identity alive across generations. Vermette's multi-voice form turns memory into structure.

Violence is racialized and gendered.

The MMIWG context shows that Indigenous women face violence shaped by colonial and patriarchal systems. This makes survival and care politically meaningful in the novel.

Literary Theory Requirement

Postcolonial theory: major ideas, theorists, and what I learned

The website uses postcolonial theory because the novel shows that colonialism is not only an event in the past. Its effects continue through family separation, social systems, cultural loss, and gendered violence.

Major idea: ongoing colonial power

Postcolonial theory asks how colonial power continues after official colonial policies appear to be over. In The Strangers, harm continues through institutions, family separation, and silence.

Major theorist: Patrick Wolfe

Wolfe's idea of settler colonialism as a logic of elimination helps explain why breaking family and cultural continuity matters. Reconnection becomes resistance because colonial power tries to weaken Indigenous presence.

Indigenous feminist context

Emma LaRocque and the MMIWG context help show that colonial violence is also gendered. The novel's focus on Indigenous women makes survival, care, and memory politically meaningful.

What I learned

I learned that survival in the novel is not just personal strength. Through a postcolonial lens, remembering family, protecting culture, and remaining present become acts of resistance against erasure.

Historical Context

From policy to family experience

Sixties Scoop

Indigenous children were removed from families and communities through child welfare systems, producing long-term loss of kinship and identity.

MMIWG

The National Inquiry links violence against Indigenous women, girls, and 2SLGBTQQIA+ people to colonial structures and institutional failure.

The novel

The Strangers transforms these public histories into intimate family narrative, showing how harm is lived across generations.

Voice reflection transcript

My website analyzes Katherena Vermette's The Strangers through a postcolonial lens. I chose this lens because the novel is not only about one family's private pain. It shows how colonial systems continue to shape Indigenous families long after the first acts of colonization. In the novel, separation between mothers, daughters, sisters, and grandmothers is connected to larger histories of child welfare, cultural disruption, addiction, violence, and silence.

One important idea in my website is that family disconnection is not random. It is part of a system. When the characters struggle to know where they belong, the novel asks readers to think about how colonialism damages identity and kinship. That is why one of the short quotations I discuss is, "I don't even know who I am." To me, this line expresses more than confusion. It shows how losing connection to family and culture can make a person feel separated from their own identity.

A second idea is cultural memory. The phrase "Like blood memory" is important because it suggests that knowledge can survive even when it has been interrupted. Language, family stories, and inherited memory are not just things from the past. They become tools for rebuilding identity in the present. This also explains why my website uses images of the book, the author, and the red dress symbol connected to MMIWG. These visuals help show that the novel's themes are connected to real histories, real communities, and real forms of remembrance.

Finally, I argue that survival itself is political in The Strangers. The women in the novel are not only victims of colonial harm. They continue to reach for family, protect each other, remember, and move forward. The line "I just want to know them" captures that desire for reconnection. Through these moments, Vermette shows that Indigenous women's presence, memory, and relationships are powerful forms of resistance. My conclusion is that The Strangers does not allow colonial violence to have the final word. Instead, the novel shows that remembering, reconnecting, and surviving can become acts of resistance against erasure.
